The Next Generation of Urban Greeners Is Already Here

Our children are watching what we do with the greenery and the responsibility we inherited.

By Jana Opperman · 13 November 2026

A child walking home from school learns which side of the street has shade. At home, they learn something else by watching the adults. Do we care for the tree that is already there? Do we give new plants water through their first summer? Do we ask why the walk from the front door is so exposed? What a child sees us make time for becomes part of what they understand a home to be.

Previous generations left us much of the city we live in. They also left us a share of its repair. In neighbourhoods built where native vegetation once stood, land was cleared for homes, roads and other infrastructure. Much of what we now ask parks to provide, from shade to habitat, was once present across a wider landscape. We can hand the next generation that same unfinished work, plus the years we chose to wait. They will learn from the choice.

A city’s greenery also grows on private land

Western Australia’s 2024 canopy release put tree canopy across the Perth and Peel monitoring area at about 22 per cent. The state has set a 30 per cent canopy target for Perth by 2040 and a goal of planting one million trees by 2035. The first figure is a reported baseline for its monitoring area. The other two are government commitments. A tree planted and a canopy established are different measures, and a regional average cannot tell a child whether their street has shade. WA’s 2024 canopy release · WA Urban Greening Strategy

The region’s long-range planning frameworks anticipate more people and homes. Every new home is a chance to decide how much living cover a neighbourhood will have when the children moving into it are adults. A mature tree removed this year cannot be replaced with equivalent shade by planting a sapling next year. The delay is paid in seasons. WA’s regional planning frameworks

The strategy covers public and private land because both are part of the city. Councils can plant and care for street trees. Developers can keep mature trees where feasible and leave usable soil for new ones. Governments can put resources into places that have the least shade. Households can care for what is on the land they use and ask for better decisions where they have a say. Our responsibilities differ with the land, money and authority available to us. Each of those responsibilities is real.

Let children see the work

Walk the route your household takes most often. Let a child tell you where the walk feels hot. Look for the tree already shading a gate or the bare edge where there is room for planting. Choose one action within reach and do it together. Care for an existing bed. Make room for a pot if that is what you can manage. If you have the space and permission, choose a suitable tree and work out who will water it while it establishes. Come back next season and look at what changed.

By 2050, some of the children making that walk today will be raising children of their own. They will live with the regional climate they inherit, and with the street-level choices we make about shade, soil and habitat. We have a duty to return living cover where we can and to care for the greenery already doing that work. We can begin while our children are still here to see us do it.
